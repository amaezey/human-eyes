## Summary
Gault reports on StoryScope, an academic preprint that distinguishes human and model-generated fiction using discourse-level narrative choices rather than only surface wording. The article accurately conveys the study's main corpus, models, and headline feature differences, then adds interviews with Jenna Russell about interpretability, Books3, AI-assisted research and writing, and the distinction readers and teachers may draw between tool use and human control of the underlying ideas. For human-eyes, its main value is accessible framing, provenance, and workflow disclosure. The reusable empirical weight belongs to the paper, not the article's deliberately provocative headline or editorial judgement that AI fiction is simply “stupid and bad.”

## Main insights
- The useful shift is from sentence-level tells to narrative construction: thematic explicitness, plot and subplot structure, character agency, temporal discontinuity, setting, references, and how emotion is rendered.
- The article reports the paper's strongest common AI tendencies: explicit moral or thematic explanation, philosophical dialogue, vague rather than named references, fewer subplots, more linear time, embodied emotion, and less narrative diversity.
- It preserves model-version fingerprints rather than presenting all LLM fiction as identical: Claude Sonnet 4.6 has flatter event escalation, GPT-5.4 over-indexes on dream sequences, and Gemini 3 Flash favours external character description.
- The interview makes interpretability a product point: a reviewer can name a tangible narrative choice such as the absence of subplots instead of returning only an opaque probability.
- The article adds provenance and ethics context not captured by the headline: the human corpus comes from Books3; the human stories are not released; the researchers disclose copyright concerns; and Russell describes using coding agents and LLMs as implementation and editing aids with manual accept/reject review.
- Russell's final distinction is about human creative control rather than zero AI contact. That supports human-eyes's anti-provenance-verdict stance: a surface or narrative finding should prompt review, not decide authorship or acceptable use by itself.

## Skill-use audit
- **Good use:** Introduce the narrative-versus-surface distinction; explain why fiction needs genre-specific review; support provenance and disclosure questions; point readers to the upstream paper and dataset; preserve the researcher interviews as context for human creative control.
- **Misuse / overclaim:** Do not cite the headline as evidence that AI fiction is uniformly bad or easy for people to detect. Do not turn the listed features into an authorship verdict or rewrite every human story toward ambiguity, flashbacks, named references, and larger casts.
- **Unsupported use:** Numeric thresholds, hard severity, transfer to short fiction or non-English fiction, transfer to later models, and claims that a single dream sequence, smell image, tidy ending, or moral statement proves model authorship.
- **Underused evidence:** The article's most useful non-paper contribution is its detailed account of AI-assisted research and editing with human review. Current project guidance treats undisclosed generated content as provenance evidence but does not describe graduated, disclosed tool use well.
- **Patterns left on the table:** Story-level thematic over-explanation, philosophical dialogue as exposition, single-track causal closure, missing subplots, low temporal discontinuity, embodied emotion and setting-as-psychological-mirror density, vague intertextual allusion, and model-specific narrative fingerprints.
