How to avoid AI voice:

Increasingly "AI voice" sounds hollow and cheap to most readers. But the truth is, everyone uses AI for writing. The trick is doing it well.

Here are some guidelines I recommend:

* Don't do AI one-shotting. That will guarantee AI voice. If you must, start from your own bullet points and use a custom prompt. Feed some your past writing into the context window so it mimics your personal writing style. (@claudeai Projects are great for this.)
* Lazy AI writing is easily detectable. A UChicago study reviewed all the leading AI detectors and found the leading detector, @pangram, can detect AI text with 99%+ precision and recall. If you are doing lazy AI writing, Pangram will catch it. AIs exhibit subtle statistical patterns (specifically, they only choose next-tokens with relatively low entropy). Even if you were to try your best to sound like an AI, it wouldn't register—human generated text has a different statistical signature.
* Run your draft through Pangram before you publish. If you run your piece through Pangram and it flags it as "AI-written," assume your readers will too. Readers will become better and better at calling this out, so get ahead of it.
* Ideally, use AI as an editor, not as a ghostwriter. Ask it to "tighten up your language" or "make this section flow better," but at the paragraph or sentence level, not the entire document.
* For full-piece edits: again, don't one-shot. First ask for an overall critique. Debate different approaches to writing the piece. Then ask for its top 10 most important edits with in-line before-and-after changes, along with explanations. Accept only the ones you agree with.
* Avoid common AI-isms: em dashes (—), "delve," "intricate," "not just X, but Y," stuff like that. It's not even that these guarantee that it's AI-written, but if you use them, you'll get annoying people in the comments assuming that it was written by AI. Especially em dashes, they are basically ruined for everyone. (Thanks AIs.)
