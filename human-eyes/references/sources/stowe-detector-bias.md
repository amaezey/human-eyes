## Summary

Stowe et al. test 16 machine-generated-text detectors for demographic bias on 41,743 human-written United States student essays, using logistic regression with essay length and perplexity covariates, dominance analysis, and intersectional subgroup tests. Detector accuracy varies sharply and detector outputs are only weakly correlated. Aggregate bias directions are inconsistent, but ELL status is the clearest recurring risk: ELL essays are more often labelled machine-generated, and the effect is concentrated among non-White ELL students. Economic-status effects are substantial but often run in an unexpected direction, while aggregate gender and race results conceal intersectional disparities. Three expert annotators perform near chance on a balanced human/generated task whose generated texts are Claude Sonnet 3.5 continuations explicitly prompted to mimic grade 6-12 student style, including possible misspellings and punctuation errors; they show no statistically significant demographic effects in this small study. The Responsible NLP Checklist adds governance limits: personal recruitment and consent are reported, but the protocol was not approved or exempted by an ethics review board; the checklist also records data/content handling statements and disclosed AI-assistant use. For human-eyes, the paper strengthens the existing no-authorship-verdict boundary and adds direct evidence for demographic, intersectional, and detector-specific evaluation; it does not validate any surface writing pattern or single-document authorship inference.

## Main insights

- The 16 detectors range from strong to worse-than-chance benchmark performance. Table 2 directly reports AUROC 0.262 for Zippy, 0.327-0.805 across BiScope domains, and 0.994 for Desklib. BiScope Essay is the strongest BiScope variant on these student essays; the authors say this is *likely* because its training domain best matches the evaluation data, so domain match is an interpretation rather than a controlled causal result.
- Detector predictions are not interchangeable. Only 5.5% of detector pairs have Pearson correlation above 0.6, and none exceed 0.8.
- The authors find only 12 of 64 detector-by-attribute observations with both corrected statistical significance and more than 5% dominance. Bias is therefore not one stable direction shared by all systems.
- ELL status is the main recurring aggregate disparity. Most detectors score ELL essays as machine-generated more often, although effect magnitudes are usually small and several model results differ.
- Intersectional analysis changes the conclusion. Seven detectors significantly over-flag non-White ELL essays, compared with one detector for White ELL essays; three additional detectors show this disparity for male non-White ELL students relative to female students.
- Race/ethnicity groups do not move uniformly. Asian/Pacific Islander essays are more often labelled machine-generated by most detectors, Black/African American essays less often, and the American Indian/Alaskan Native group is too small (n=208) to interpret a null result as no bias.
- Economic disadvantage produces a strong but mixed result. Many BiScope, Fast-DetectGPT, and trained variants label students without economic disadvantage as machine-generated more often, while some zero-shot/fine-tuned systems move in the opposite direction.
- Higher detector AUROC is descriptively associated with lower regression pseudo-R2 bias (r≈-0.486), but the paper does not establish causation.
- Three expert annotators achieve 0.449-0.526 accuracy and no statistically significant attribute effects on this style-matched continuation task. The result is preliminary because there are only three annotators, each sees a subset, and the generated comparison texts are Claude Sonnet 3.5 continuations explicitly prompted to emulate grade 6-12 writing and permitted to include misspellings and punctuation errors.
- The evidence is high-stakes but narrow: English persuasive essays from United States grades 6-12; inherited binary gender and fixed race categories; public detector versions available during the study; one fairness method; and no claim of transfer to other languages, ages, genres, populations, or detector releases.
- Reproducibility and governance are qualified: Appendix B gives repository URLs but not exact commits/checkpoint hashes, and the checklist says the annotator protocol had no ethics-review approval or exemption, while reporting personal recruitment, salaried compensation, consent, data/content handling statements, and use of AI assistance in language and short-form input.

## Evidence and claims to extract

- **Direct source reviewed:** Complete 13-page ACL 2026 proceedings PDF, DOI 10.18653/v1/2026.acl-long.109, including all sections, Tables 1-7, Figures 1-2, footnotes, limitations, references, and Appendices A-E; and the complete two-page first-party ACL Responsible NLP Checklist linked to the paper.
- **Method and sample:** The authors deduplicated PERSUADE 2.0, ASAP 2.0, and ELLIPSE and removed rows with missing demographics, yielding 41,743 human essays: 21,277 male/20,466 female; 15,078 White/26,665 non-White; 31,079 non-ELL/10,664 ELL; and 18,188 not economically disadvantaged/23,555 disadvantaged. Detector benchmark data came from OUTFOX, which pairs PERSUADE essays with three generated counterparts. Bias regressions predict detector error from gender, race/ethnicity, ELL status, and economic status while controlling for OPT-IML-1.3B perplexity and whitespace word count; all reported VIF values are below 4. Significance uses p=.01 with Bonferroni correction; dominance above 5% is treated as meaningful. Sixteen intersectional subgroups are compared by pairwise z-tests at corrected p<3.91e-5, and individual race/ethnicity groups at corrected p<1.04e-4. Human evaluation balances 800 examples across attributes and human/generated labels; Claude Sonnet 3.5 continues the first 20 tokens of each selected essay under a prompt to emulate grade 6-12 student style, stay under the original length, and optionally include common misspellings and punctuation errors; three expert annotators classify subsets of 231-318 texts.
- **Direct versus cited evidence:** C01-C09 are results, methods, limitations, checklist disclosures, or practitioner conclusions measured or stated by Stowe et al. The paper discusses Liang et al. (2023), Verma et al. (2024), and other prior work, but this card does not use those inherited claims as new direct evidence. Existing project card `liang-detector-bias.md` is a separate full-text-reviewed record of the 2023 TOEFL/eighth-grade study; its evidence remains distinct from this ingestion.
- **Benchmark result detail:** Table 2 directly reports AUROC 0.667 Ghostbuster, 0.948 Glimpse, 0.907 Binoculars, 0.708/0.829 Fast-DetectGPT, 0.262 Zippy, 0.726/0.327/0.805/0.362 BiScope, 0.477/0.696/0.450 DeTeCtive, 0.706 RADAR, 0.994 Desklib, and 0.362 E5-lora. The authors interpret BiScope Essay's within-family lead as likely due to training-domain match; this was not a controlled causal test. Appendix B identifies repositories, describes four locally trained BiScope variants, and notes a Ghostbuster modification, but provides no exact commit identifiers or detector checkpoint hashes. Results are corpus-, threshold-, implementation-, and version-specific, not a ranking guaranteed to transfer.
- **Regression result detail:** Only 12/64 observations combine corrected significance with dominance above 5%. ELL is the clearest recurring negative effect; economic status is important but mixed; aggregate race and gender effects appear limited until subgroup analysis.
- **Intersectional result detail:** Non-White ELL n=9,443 versus White ELL n=1,221; seven detectors significantly over-flag the former and one the latter. Three additional detectors show significant differences for male non-White ELL essays. A/PI essays trend toward more machine-generated labels, B/AA toward fewer, while AI/AN n=208 is underpowered.
- **Human result detail:** Annotator accuracies are 0.492, 0.449, and 0.526. No gender, race/ethnicity, ELL, or economic-status coefficient reaches p<.01. This result is confined to the 800-item balanced setup in which the generated half consists of Claude Sonnet 3.5 continuations explicitly style-matched to grade 6-12 student writing, including permission for misspellings and punctuation errors.
- **Checklist governance detail:** The checklist says three annotators were personally recruited, compensated through salaried work above minimum wage, and consented to individual use of their results; it also says the collection protocol was not approved or determined exempt by an ethics review board. For scientific artifacts, the authors state that they do not release or modify the data, that it contains no identifying information, and that potentially offensive content may remain because all text types are needed for analysis. The checklist records AI assistance for language and short-form input, while marking the detailed-use question “no.” These disclosures qualify governance and reproducibility rather than altering the detector-bias results.
- **Important limits and counterexamples:** Bias direction differs by model and model variant; economically disadvantaged essays are often less likely to be flagged; many significant coefficients have weak dominance; race/gender aggregate conclusions change under intersectional analysis; AI/AN null findings are underpowered; the accuracy-bias correlation is descriptive; controlling for perplexity and length does not establish that either causes a demographic disparity; humans are not shown to be accurate detectors outside the style-matched continuation setup; exact detector commits/checkpoints are unavailable; the annotator protocol lacked ethics-review approval/exemption; and the study cannot justify conclusions outside its English United States school-essay setting.

## Skill-use audit

- **Good use:** Support the hard boundary against authorship classification; justify warning language around false positives; require detector- and subgroup-specific fairness analysis before deployment claims; add demographic/intersectional coverage to evaluation design; and preserve model, corpus, language, age, genre, and version metadata when discussing detector evidence.
- **Misuse / overclaim:** Do not cite the paper as evidence that any human-eyes phrase, rhythm, or structure check detects AI authorship. Do not turn an ELL disparity into a list of ELL-language features to flag or rewrite. Do not claim that every detector discriminates in the same direction.
- **Unsupported use:** A universal false-positive rate; transfer to adult, workplace, academic, journalistic, creative, non-US, or non-English writing; causal claims about why a demographic effect occurs; a guarantee that stronger AUROC removes bias; or a claim that human review is accurate or bias-free in general.
- **Underused evidence:** Current project evaluation varies genre/register and reports false positives, but it does not explicitly stratify human false positives by ELL status, race/ethnicity, gender, economic status, or intersections, and the committed corpus is too small for such estimates.
- **Patterns left on the table:** None as prose patterns. The source adds evaluation and deployment safeguards, not a writing-tell taxonomy.

## Matched patterns / rules

- **Product boundary:** `human-eyes/SKILL.md` says the skill identifies constructions and never classifies authorship; `STRATEGY.md` says it examines writing rather than determining whether a human or AI wrote it; `human-eyes/references/process.md` prohibits authorship statements. These directly address the principal misuse risk.
- **README caution:** `README.md` describes human-eyes as a pattern detector rather than an AI detector, warns that human writing can be wrongly flagged, and already maps Liang et al. only to false-positive caution. Stowe et al. provide broader 2026 and intersectional support for that stance.

- **Student genre assessment:** `human-eyes/scripts/judgement.json` checks student essays for rubric echoing, evidence quality, baseline shifts, surface polish, and agency. It does not classify authorship or use demographic proxies; no source result supports adding them.
- **Testing:** `dev/TESTING.md` requires false positives and genre/format confounds in benchmark reports and explicitly says human-eyes measures prose patterns rather than authorship. It broadens authors, genres, registers, eras, and source types, but it does not yet name demographic or intersectional false-positive auditing.
- **No deterministic rule coverage:** `human-eyes/scripts/patterns.json`, `human-eyes/references/patterns.md`, and the grader checks contain no demographic classifier and should not acquire one from this paper.

## Associated hypotheses

- **H1 continuous calibrated register-distance score:** The paper reinforces the need to report subgroup reliability rather than only an overall score, but it does not validate human-eyes pattern z-scores.
- **H3 drop detection framing entirely:** Project inference, not a direct paper recommendation. The paper's practitioner conclusion is that detection models require case-by-case scrutiny and that misclassification risk must be understood; human-eyes may reasonably treat this as additional support for its stricter no-authorship-verdict boundary, but Stowe et al. do not argue that detection framing must be dropped entirely.
- **H9 field-guide look-alike disambiguation:** Project inference, not a causal finding. The paper documents ELL and intersectional disparities after controlling for perplexity and length, which supports caution around demographic/register look-alikes. It does not show that surface fluency, perplexity, or any particular prose feature causes those disparities.
- **H12 genre-aware threshold calibration:** Student-essay/domain matching matters, but the paper demonstrates detector domain sensitivity rather than validating human-eyes genre thresholds.
- **H19 bootstrap confidence intervals:** Consistent with reporting uncertainty and subgroup power, especially where a small group such as AI/AN cannot support a no-bias conclusion.
- **Potential new hypothesis:** A human-eyes evaluation that appears acceptable in aggregate may conceal subgroup or intersectional false-positive disparities; demographic fairness requires sufficiently powered, consented, appropriately governed data rather than proxy inference from prose.

## Questions / follow-up

- Can future licensed human corpora include consented ELL and demographic metadata at enough scale to evaluate subgroup false-positive rates without inferring sensitive attributes from text?
- Should `dev/TESTING.md` require a fairness section when evaluation data contains demographic attributes, while explicitly prohibiting demographic inference when it does not?
- Should H3/H9 be updated to name intersectional detector effects rather than only non-native-English false positives?
- Can exact repository commits, detector checkpoint hashes, and reproducible subgroup splits be recovered from the authors' code/data release for a separate replication review? Appendix B provides repository URLs but not those identifiers, and the linked artifacts were not recursively ingested.
- How do the same 16 systems behave on adult, workplace, multilingual, or non-persuasive writing and after post-2026 model updates?

## Recommendations

- **C01:** Keep the direct detector AUROCs as corpus-, implementation-, threshold-, and version-specific background; label domain match as the authors' qualified interpretation, and require exact commits/checkpoints for replication. Never present the numbers as human-eyes performance.
- **C02:** Add detector disagreement only to future evidence/evaluation guidance, with the study scope attached; do not make it a prose rule.
- **C03:** Use Stowe et al. to strengthen the existing ELL false-positive caution while preserving system heterogeneity and effect size.
- **C04:** Require intersectional analysis only when future evaluation has consented demographic labels, adequate power, privacy review, and explicit governance; never infer sensitive attributes from prose.
- **C05:** Require subgroup sample counts and uncertainty before interpreting a null result as absence of bias.
- **C06:** Preserve the mixed and counter-directional economic-status finding as evidence; take no detector or prose-rule action.
- **C07:** Keep the AUROC-bias correlation hypothesis-generating and non-causal unless independently replicated.
- **C08:** Record that unaided human guessing is inaccurate in this grade 6-12 style-matched continuation setup and does not provide a general replacement for automated detection; retain the checklist's recruitment, consent, compensation, and absent ethics-review approval/exemption limitations.
- **C09:** Consider a fairness subsection in evaluation guidance that distinguishes measured subgroup fairness from unmeasured fairness, prohibits demographic proxy inference, and requires exact detector-version plus human-subjects governance metadata for replication.
