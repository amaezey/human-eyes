## Summary

Edwards reports Sam Altman's 2025-11-14 X claim that ChatGPT had begun obeying a request in custom instructions to avoid em dashes, places the claim two days after the GPT-5.1 release, and describes one undocumented Ars test that appeared to comply. The article also preserves the opposite cases: humans used em dashes heavily before ChatGPT, two linked in-chat screenshots show ChatGPT still violating the request, the reason for any model tendency is unknown, and updates may change behaviour. For human-eyes this is useful dated journalism about public-tell drift, prompt surface, human look-alikes, and non-deterministic compliance. It is not a frequency study, a GPT-5.1 evaluation, evidence for C7's any-occurrence strong warning, proof of a training or RLHF mechanism, or authorship evidence.

## Main insights

- Altman's directly checked post says avoidance worked when placed in **custom instructions**; it does not say ChatGPT suppresses em dashes by default.
- Edwards' own GPT-5.1 check is explicitly informal and reports only that ChatGPT “did appear” to comply. No raw input, output, version/build, settings, repeated trials, or comparison is supplied.
- The two preserved failure screenshots concern instructions given **in chat**, not custom instructions. They qualify the broader reliability discussion but do not contradict the narrower surface-specific claim on equivalent conditions.
- The article offers strong human-look-alike context: pre-ChatGPT writers discussed overusing em dashes, and one cited writer described using them in every paragraph.

- The article's most supportable project lesson is drift: behaviour can depend on model/product version, instruction surface, and mutable tuning, so source date and version matter.
