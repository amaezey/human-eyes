## Summary

Phillips argues that em-dash presence is an invalid standalone shortcut for identifying AI-generated prose and that writers should not abandon deliberate punctuation to avoid public suspicion. The article supplies a complete, bylined professional published-prose control with 16 em dashes, three described rhetorical functions, an explicit overuse qualification, and a direct null statement that the author has seen no hard evidence for higher chatbot use. The byline establishes publication attribution, not a separately verified production process. This is forceful cultural criticism and craft reasoning, not a frequency study, model test, detector evaluation, causal analysis, or proof of human-only authorship. Its strongest contribution to human-eyes is a false-positive caution and anti-camouflage argument that challenge the live fail-on-any behavior while leaving the need for matched calibration unresolved.

## Main insights

- An em dash in a text is not, by itself, evidence that AI generated the text; a one-feature filter would produce both human false positives and AI false negatives.
- Phillips describes three legitimate functions: interrupting a thought with a modification, emphasizing a conclusion, and marking a pause or hitch. He also says overuse can be annoying, so the defense is not a claim that every use is good.
- Phillips says he has seen no hard evidence that chatbots use more em dashes than anyone else. Reports that a chatbot may ignore a no-dash instruction are anecdotal and do not prove lower human frequency.
- The article interprets any chatbot predilection as inheritance from human-authored training text. Its training-data and Meta-book claims are partly inherited through linked reporting, not measured in this article.
- The origin of the online em-dash theory is described as disputed. The article links several competing origin accounts and dates the viral discourse only approximately to February 2025.
- Phillips argues that complex grammar can support complex thought and that contemporary pressure toward grammatical minimalism can sometimes flatten ideas. This is a craft and cultural judgment, not a measured cognitive result.
- The em dash is presented as a discretionary tool for adding, turning, and qualifying thought without making a sentence inaccessible.
- Avoiding em dashes solely to evade AI suspicion is characterized as harmful anti-AI camouflage: it can remove deliberate voice and make human writing less natural.
- The preserved, bylined professional article contains 16 U+2014 em dashes across seven of 16 body paragraphs, supplying one direct published-prose control but no independently verified authorship process, population frequency, or acceptable-density threshold.
- Phillips also asserts that chatbots lack consciousness and style, generate through probabilistic associations, and lack a reliable way to compare output with truth. These are direct author explanations without technical evidence in this article, not findings human-eyes should adopt as a mechanism.
