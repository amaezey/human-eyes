## Summary

Russell, Karpinska, and Iyyer compare selected frequent LLM users, four less-experienced annotators, commercial and open-source classifiers, and prompted LLM detectors on 300 paired American-English nonfiction articles. A majority of five pre-screened frequent users misclassifies one article, but individual performance varies sharply and one expert fails completely on the humanized o1-Pro condition. The final humanizer was selected after several post-hoc routes were abandoned through first-author qualitative judgment and a framework was tuned until it could evade that author's own detection, then rebuilt as one-step generation using the experts' guide and same-publication examples. The paper's main prose contribution is a model-coded taxonomy of what those reviewers mentioned in explanations, not a controlled per-cue validation: category frequencies measure mentions regardless of correctness, many cues also appear in false positives, and several were built into the generation or evasion prompts. The full paper supports cautious, dated evaluation and review-design evidence plus candidate dimensions and examples. It does not validate the old card's blanket B3, B4, or C3 mappings, any live human-eyes threshold, a generic authorship detector, or a claim about ordinary readers.

## Main insights

- The five-expert majority vote reports 99.3% TPR and 0% FPR overall, with one false negative among 300 articles. This is a selected upper-bound sample, not a population estimate.
- Individual results are much less uniform: overall expert TPRs range from 59.3% to 97.3%, one expert's humanized-o1 TPR is 0%, and individual overall FPRs range from 1.3% to 12%.
- Four nonexperts perform near chance despite high confidence. The paper internally disagrees on both Experiment 1 average FPRs: Table 1 gives 51.7% for nonexperts and 4.0% for experts, while the body gives 52.5% and 3.3%; the five expert Table 2 values average 3.34%.
- Expert majority votes remain perfect on GPT-4o, Claude, GPT-4o paraphrased, and humanized o1-Pro batches, and miss one unhumanized o1-Pro article. These are five 30-positive/30-negative batches, not repeated trials or unseen-population estimates.
- Table 2 shows Pangram Humanizers matching the expert majority's 99.3% overall TPR with 2.7% FPR, while base Pangram reports 98.0% TPR and 2.0% FPR. The body then calls 2.7% the FPR “for base model,” contradicting the displayed model labels. Other evaluated automatic and prompted detectors degrade more under paraphrasing or humanization.
- The explanation taxonomy was developed by the first two authors from a stratified 25-comment sample, then assigned across 1,500 explanations by GPT-4o-2024-11-20 after prompt iteration. The paper reports no held-out coding accuracy, agreement statistic, confusion matrix, or category-assignment uncertainty.
- Category percentages count explanations mentioning a category regardless of whether the associated decision was correct. They therefore describe reviewer attention, not each cue's precision, recall, specificity, causal role, or independent predictive value.
- Vocabulary is the most-mentioned category (53.1%), followed by sentence structure (35.9%), grammar and punctuation (24.8%), originality (23.7%), quotations (22.3%), and clarity (19.5%). The remaining reported categories are formatting (15.0%), conclusions (13.1%), formality (12.3%), names and titles (11.7%), tone (9.3%), introductions (7.3%), factuality (7.2%), topics (3.1%), and other (2.6%).
- Exact candidates include `vibrant`, `crucial`, `significantly`, `testament`, `not only ... but also ...`, `it's not just this, it's this`, three-item lists, optimistic summary endings, repeated Emily/Sarah names, uniform professional titles, similar-looking paragraphs, consistent headings, and homogenized quotations. These remain source- and condition-bound reviewer cues.
- The paper also preserves human look-alikes and nulls: 31% of false-positive explanations mention vocabulary; human articles contain words such as `delve` and `crucial`; sentence structure and formality mislead reviewers; neutral tone misleads nonexperts; contractions and colloquialisms fool one expert; factuality was not a significant cue; and one long, highly formulaic o1-Pro article defeats the majority.
- Released explanations disagree about punctuation direction and granularity. Some experts treat em dashes, long dashes, punctuation variety, or an open/spaced en dash as human evidence; another says AI uses many em dashes and calls them “obligatory” in an AI example; one distinguishes spaced en dashes from closed em dashes. Table 3 collapses this into AI “avoiding dashes and ellipses.” This bidirectional record supplies human look-alike counterevidence to a fail-on-any C7 rule, not a clean opposite-direction em-dash rule or replacement threshold.
- Paraphrasing and the selected custom humanizer do not defeat the majority in these batches, but the humanizer has material tuning leakage: authors tried and qualitatively rejected generate-then-humanize, iterative step-by-step, and two-step post-hoc routes; selected an approach after it could evade the first author's own detection; and then moved to one-step generation using the experts' guide and same-publication human/AI examples. The abandoned routes are qualitative nulls, and the final batch is not evidence that humans are robust to arbitrary current humanizers.
- Prompted GPT-4o and o1 detectors do not reproduce the human majority, especially on humanized o1-Pro. The released detector code is an API wrapper around the guide and prompt templates, not a trained or independently calibrated human-eyes-style assessment.
- The story pilot has an 81.3% average FPR and deliberately mismatched Reddit-native human prose against more formal generated prose. The authors treat it as a reason to narrow the main study, not positive evidence for fiction detection.
- The complete repository preserves 300 records and all five experimental conditions, but one expert annotation is absent and paper/release counts require qualification: the introduction says 1,790 total annotations, Methods says 1,740 from nine annotators, and the release contains 1,499 nonempty expert annotations. The extra 50 is consistent with the ten five-item screening trials described in Appendix A.1, but the paper does not explicitly reconcile the headline counts. Reproduction is also blocked on released paths: `utils.py` requires an absent initial-sentence paraphrase prompt, and `models.py` can read an unassigned `key` when no credential argument is supplied.

## Matched patterns / rules

- B1 `no-ai-vocabulary-clustering` and `overall-signal-stacking`: partial lexical overlap. Focused execution on the source-described sentence `The vibrant community made a crucial discovery that significantly changed the landscape.` flags `vibrant`, `crucial`, and `landscape`, but not `significantly`; the source supplies no three-per-paragraph threshold or structural-stacking validation.
- B3 `no-negative-parallelisms`: recognizes the source-exact `It's not just this, it's this.` as one candidate. The source reports reviewer mentions and false positives, not a severity or document threshold.
- B4 `no-forced-triads`, and `formulaic_parallelism`: the focused check recognizes `speed, safety, and cost` as one triad; B4 does not issue a verdict below 300 words or below 4.0 triads per 1000 words. The source does not validate those thresholds and says sentence-structure fixation can cause false positives.
- E4 `no-generic-conclusions`, H2 `no-tidy-paragraph-endings`, and `no-signposted-conclusions`: partial coverage of optimistic, summarizing, and overly neat endings. The focused exact catalogue sentence `The future looks bright for renewable energy.` fires E4, but the source's category is broader and includes human counterexamples.
- `paragraph-length-uniformity`, G3 `no-excessive-lists`, `no-inline-header-lists`, and G6 `no-section-scaffolding`: partial coverage of similar paragraph lengths, bolded list-header formatting, and repeated labels. C3 title case is not source coverage: the paper names ALL-CAPS or consistently formatted headings, not title case.
- H3 `tonal_uniformity`, H7 `neutrality_collapse`, `referential_clarity`, `semantic_redundancy`, and H28: partial higher-level coverage for tone, formality, clarity, and repetition. None reproduces the study's source-bound comparison task or cue coding.
- H10 `genre_specific` journalism branch: partial coverage for unverifiable quotations, named sources, dates, links, and factual provenance. It does not assess quote-style homogeneity, generic names, uniform titles, or the source's full article-level cue set.
- C7 `no-em-dashes`: challenged by bidirectional, reviewer-specific evidence. Records 32, 36, and 52 include experts treating em dashes as human evidence; records 6 and 168 include one expert treating many or “obligatory” em dashes as AI evidence; record 1 distinguishes a spaced en dash from an em dash. Table 3 compresses this disagreement into avoidance of dashes/ellipses as AI. The source supplies human look-alikes to fail-on-any behavior, not a safe direction or alternative threshold.
- `human-eyes/references/process.md` and `dev/TESTING.md`: fully cover the no-authorship product boundary, complete-Audit distinction, matched-register and provenance requirements, human look-alikes, weak/reversed cases, and separation of candidate recognition from threshold firing.

## Associated hypotheses

- H28 originality, clarity, and formality as comparison dimensions: directly motivated and expanded to the full 15-category explanation taxonomy, with false-positive and reviewer-disagreement controls.
- H3 drop detection framing entirely: informed by the gap between a selected five-person majority and transferable document-level claims.
- H9 field-guide disambiguation: informed by vocabulary, sentence-structure, neutrality, formality, and punctuation false positives.
- H12 genre-aware threshold calibration: directly informed by the nonfiction scope and failed, mismatched Reddit-story pilot.
- H16 multi-judge ensemble: indirectly informed by divergent reviewer focus and majority improvement, although this source studies humans rather than model judges.
- H24 register-specific vocabulary density and H25 model-family residue: informed by model/version, paraphrase, humanization, publication, and cue-drift conditions, but the paper measures cue mentions rather than word frequencies.
