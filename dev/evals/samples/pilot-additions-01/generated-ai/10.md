The national survey provides new information about how Australian patients experience coordination between different parts of the health system. Respondents were asked about communication among health professionals, follow-up after hospital care, access to information and the support they received in managing ongoing conditions. The results show that many patients experienced well-connected care, but coordination was not consistent across the population.

Most respondents reported that their usual health professional was aware of important aspects of their medical history. Patients also commonly said that test results and specialist information were available during appointments. These findings indicate that information sharing works effectively for many people, particularly those who have an established relationship with a regular general practitioner.

However, gaps were more apparent when patients moved between services. Some respondents said they had to repeat their medical history because information had not reached a new provider. Others reported that they were uncertain which professional was responsible for follow-up. After a hospital admission, a proportion of patients did not receive timely advice about medicines, warning signs or the next stage of care. These experiences can cause inconvenience and anxiety and may increase the risk of duplicated tests or avoidable treatment.

Patients with complex needs had more contact with the health system and therefore more opportunities to encounter coordination problems. People managing multiple chronic conditions, those using both health and disability services, and patients who saw several specialists were more likely to report mixed experiences. Survey responses also varied by socioeconomic circumstances, location and ability to communicate confidently with providers. These differences warrant further investigation because poor coordination can compound existing barriers to care.

Continuity was associated with better reported experiences. Patients who could see the same general practitioner or attend the same practice were more likely to say that professionals understood their needs and helped organise referrals. Clear explanations were also important. Respondents rated their care more positively when they knew whom to contact, why a referral had been made and what should happen next.

The survey measures patients’ perceptions rather than clinical outcomes and does not establish why particular failures occurred. Nevertheless, patient reports identify practical points at which coordination succeeds or breaks down. They complement administrative and clinical data by showing how arrangements are experienced by the people using them.

The findings support continued work to improve secure information exchange, discharge communication and accountability for follow-up. They also underline the value of regular primary-care relationships. Future survey cycles will allow changes to be monitored and help determine whether improvement efforts are producing more consistent experiences across patient groups.
