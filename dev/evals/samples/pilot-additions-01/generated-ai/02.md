The computer arrived in three cardboard boxes when I was fifteen. One held the monitor, a beige block as deep as a kitchen cupboard. Another held the tower, and the third was crowded with cables, manuals, and plastic-wrapped discs. My father put everything on the dining table and announced that I would know what to do because I was young.

I did not know what to do. Until then, my experience of computers had been limited to school machines, where the programs were already open and a teacher hovered nearby. At home, every socket looked like a test. I matched plugs by shape, tightened two tiny screws on the monitor cable, and kept the manual open beside me as if it were a map out of the wilderness.

When the machine finally started, it made a confident chime that felt undeserved. The screen filled with icons whose purposes I could only guess. I learned by clicking, retreating, and clicking again. Mistakes seemed catastrophic because I did not yet understand which actions could be undone. A warning box could stop my breath. Sometimes I switched the computer off and began again, hoping a restart would erase both the problem and the evidence of my ignorance.

The internet came through the telephone line. Connecting meant listening to a sequence of beeps, static, and electronic shrieks while the modem negotiated with some distant machine. Nobody could call the house while I was online. My mother would lift the receiver, hear the noise, and shout down the hallway for me to disconnect.

There were no video tutorials waiting to answer every question. Pages loaded a strip at a time, and downloading a small program could occupy an evening. I relied on library books, magazine columns, and a patient classmate who wrote instructions on loose paper. I kept those pages in a folder: how to install software, how to change a setting, how to find a file I had saved in the wrong place.

Gradually, the computer stopped feeling like a sealed object and became a place I could explore. I learned basic HTML by viewing the source of simple websites. I changed colours, broke layouts, and rebuilt them. My first web page had a dark blue background, bright yellow text, and a row of animated stars. I thought it looked magnificent.

What I remember most is not the slowness of the technology but the slowness of learning it. Each discovery stayed with me because it had taken effort. I had to tolerate confusion long enough for it to become curiosity. Years later, when new devices became easier to use, I sometimes missed that awkward machine. It taught me that not knowing was not a verdict. It was simply the first stage of finding out.
