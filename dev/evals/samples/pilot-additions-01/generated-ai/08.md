At university, money is often discussed as though it were a private matter, separate from academic life. In my experience, it organised academic life. It decided whether I attended an early tutorial after a closing shift, whether I bought the prescribed edition of a textbook, and whether an invitation to “grab lunch” felt casual or impossible. I learned the language of extensions, participation and opportunity alongside another, less visible curriculum: where to find microwaves, which campus computers stayed open late, and how long I could delay topping up my transport card.

I did not initially describe myself as a low-income student. That phrase sounded administrative, like a box on a scholarship form. At home, careful spending was ordinary. We compared supermarket prices, repaired things and avoided waste. University made these practices newly conspicuous. Around classmates who spoke about unpaid internships, overseas study and moving closer to campus, my calculations began to feel like evidence that I did not fully belong. I became skilled at giving socially acceptable reasons for absence. I was “busy” rather than working; I “already had plans” rather than lacking the money for a ticket.

This concealment complicates the university’s promise of equal opportunity. Formally, I had access to the same lectures, library and assessment tasks as everyone else. Materially, access was uneven. A class scheduled outside my usual days required another fare. Group work assumed that evenings were available. Networking events rewarded students who could remain on campus without losing paid hours. Even recommendations intended to help—volunteer, join a society, build a portfolio—often treated time as free. The ideal student appeared independent of rent, food and family obligations.

Yet I do not want to turn this account into a simple story of deprivation or resilience. Low income constrained me, but it also shaped forms of knowledge that the institution rarely recognised. I could plan precisely, notice hidden costs and understand why “small” administrative obstacles drive people away. I formed friendships through shared food, borrowed books and frank conversations about work. These practices were not romantic solutions to inequality; they were ways of living within it.

Writing autoethnographically requires me to connect these memories to a wider structure rather than present hardship as an individual test of character. My embarrassment was personal, but it was produced in an environment where middle-class resources were treated as neutral. The silence around money protected that norm. Because nobody had to announce that they could afford an internship or a coffee, their participation appeared to result only from motivation.

When universities measure inclusion primarily through admission, they miss what happens after a student arrives. Belonging depends not only on being offered a place but on having the time, money and security required to occupy it. My degree was shaped by intellectual discovery, but also by constant negotiation between study and survival. Recognising that negotiation changes the question from whether low-income students can adapt to university to how university life might stop demanding that they hide the conditions under which they learn.
