During 2018–19, the national innovation advisory body focused on improving the conditions in which Australian businesses, researchers and public institutions turn ideas into economic and social value. Its work combined advice to government with practical engagement across the innovation system. The central concern was not innovation as an end in itself, but its contribution to productivity, employment, competitiveness and community wellbeing.

A major priority was strengthening the links between research and industry. Australia produces high-quality research, yet too many promising findings fail to move beyond the laboratory or university. The body examined barriers to collaboration, including different incentives, limited mobility between sectors and the difficulty faced by smaller firms in identifying research partners. It supported approaches that make collaboration easier and encourage businesses to invest in research, development and commercialisation.

The body also considered how Australia could respond to technological change. Developments in automation, data analytics, artificial intelligence and advanced manufacturing were reshaping established industries and creating new markets. Advice during the year emphasised the need for firms to adopt new technologies, for workers to have opportunities to update their skills and for regulation to remain effective without preventing useful experimentation. Particular attention was given to the capacity of small and medium enterprises, which often face greater financial and managerial constraints than larger organisations.

Another area of work was the availability of finance for innovative ventures. Early-stage companies may have valuable intellectual property and strong growth prospects but little revenue or collateral. The body consulted investors, entrepreneurs and industry representatives about gaps in the funding pathway and the conditions needed to attract patient capital. It also considered the role of government programs in addressing genuine market failures while avoiding unnecessary duplication.

Regional participation remained an important theme. Innovation activity is often concentrated in major cities, but regional Australia has significant strengths in agriculture, resources, health, tourism and environmental management. The body sought evidence about how digital infrastructure, local leadership and connections to national networks could help regional firms and communities develop and adopt new solutions.

Throughout the year, members met with businesses, universities, research organisations, investors, unions and government agencies. These discussions helped test assumptions and ensured that advice reflected practical experience. The body continued to promote better use of data and evaluation so that public support could be directed towards measures that deliver demonstrable results.

In 2019–20, the body will build on this work by refining its advice on research translation, technology adoption, workforce capability and investment. Its aim remains a more connected and responsive innovation system in which more Australians can participate and benefit.
