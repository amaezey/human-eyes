When the poet’s work crossed the border, it did not arrive intact. Translation selected from it, rearranged its emphases and supplied it with a new literary ancestry. Readers in the receiving country encountered not simply a foreign writer but a figure assembled by translators, editors and critics for local purposes.

The earliest translations favoured the shorter lyrics. Their compression made them practical for magazines, while their images of night, solitude and ruined landscapes suited contemporary tastes. Longer poems, political satires and passages dense with regional speech were omitted or softened. As a result, a poet known at home for tonal variety appeared abroad as a consistently melancholy visionary. The selection was already an interpretation.

Language intensified the change. The poet’s irregular syntax and abrupt shifts between formal and colloquial diction had unsettled first readers. Translators often regularised these features, partly to achieve fluency and partly because the receiving culture valued polish as evidence of poetic seriousness. Rhyme was sometimes added where the original relied on rhythm and internal echo. These versions were elegant, but their elegance altered the poet’s voice. Difficulty became refinement; irony could sound sincere.

Reception then fed back into later translation. Critics compared the poet with a celebrated national tradition, emphasising spiritual inwardness and treating historical references as universal symbols. New editions adopted that framing in prefaces, notes and cover copy. Translators chose poems that confirmed it. The foreign reputation thus acquired a circular force: the poet was translated as a mystic because critics called him one, and critics found a mystic in translations designed around that expectation.

This transformation should not be dismissed as mere distortion. The translated work generated forms of attention unavailable in its first context. Its political disappointments could speak to readers living through different upheavals, and its experiments influenced younger poets who could not read the original language. Reception created a second career, with real artistic consequences.

But that career belonged to a collaborative construction. The poet abroad was made through omission, analogy and linguistic choice. To trace that making is to see translation not as a transparent vehicle between fixed works, but as one of the places where literary identity itself is produced.
