An Open Letter to the Country I Chose

Dear neighbours,

I have lived here long enough to know the back roads, complain about the weather with conviction, and recognise half the faces at the Saturday market. I know which bus is usually late and which bakery sells out before noon. My children learned to ride their bikes on this street. I have voted in council elections, planted trees beside the oval, and stood in the school hall selling raffle tickets beside people whose grandparents were born here.

This is my adopted country, but “adopted” does not mean temporary or borrowed. It describes a decision I made and have kept making: to build a life here, accept responsibilities here, and care about what happens beyond my own front gate.

That is why I cannot stay quiet about the way some of our neighbours are being treated.

Over the past year, I have watched a family nearby become the target of complaints that others on our street never seem to attract. Their bins are photographed if they remain on the kerb for an extra hour. Their visitors are described as suspicious. Their children are blamed for noise even when several households are outside. When they renovated their porch, people demanded to know whether every permit was in order. Similar work on another house was greeted with compliments.

None of these incidents looks enormous on its own. That is partly how unfairness survives. It arrives as a raised eyebrow, an anonymous report, a rule enforced for one person and overlooked for another. Anyone who objects is told they are imagining a pattern. Yet those who live inside the pattern feel its weight every day.

I know the hesitation that comes with speaking about this. As an immigrant, I have sometimes wondered whether gratitude requires silence. I was welcomed here. I found work, friendship, and safety. Should I not simply be thankful?

But gratitude and criticism are not opposites. If I care for this country, I must care when its promises are applied unevenly. Belonging cannot depend on pretending that everything is fair. In fact, the freedom to question what happens in our community is one of the reasons I chose to make my home here.

For years, I confused good citizenship with being agreeable. I attended community events, paid my taxes, volunteered when asked, and tried not to make anyone uncomfortable. Those things matter, but they are not the whole of civic life. Participation also means asking who is missing from the meeting, who is being spoken about rather than spoken with, and whose concerns are dismissed before they are heard.

Recently, I began attending council sessions. At first I sat near the back and said nothing. The procedures were unfamiliar, the language formal, and the confident regulars seemed to know one another. Then an issue arose about access to a public space used by several migrant families. I submitted a question. My hands shook when I read it aloud, but I read it.

Nothing changed immediately. There was no dramatic apology or unanimous vote. What changed was that the record now included a perspective that would otherwise have been absent. Other residents approached me afterward. One had noticed the same unequal treatment. Another disagreed with me but wanted to understand. A third asked how to submit a question of her own.

That small experience corrected something in me. Civic participation is not reserved for experts, lifelong residents, or the loudest voice in the room. It belongs to all of us. We do not have to wait until we feel completely certain or fully accepted before taking part.

So I am asking my fellow residents to look closely at what is happening around us. Before making a complaint, ask whether you would respond the same way if another neighbour did the same thing. When rumours circulate, speak to the person concerned rather than adding another layer of suspicion. If a meeting affects people who are not present, help make the invitation clearer, the time more accessible, or the atmosphere less hostile. Learn how local decisions are made. Write to a representative. Attend one council session. Support someone who is nervous about speaking.

I am also asking our institutions to do more than celebrate diversity in speeches and photographs. Fair treatment requires transparent processes, consistent enforcement, accessible information, and serious attention to patterns in complaints. It requires listening before defensiveness takes over.

I do not write this because I have lost faith in my adopted country. I write because my faith has matured. I no longer think belonging is a gift that can be withdrawn whenever I become inconvenient. It is a relationship, sustained by contribution, honesty, and mutual care.

This country became mine through ordinary acts: work, friendship, mourning, celebration, voting, helping, and being helped. I want it to be a place where my neighbours can claim the same belonging without enduring a daily test of whether they deserve it.

With hope and determination,

A neighbour and fellow citizen
